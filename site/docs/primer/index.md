# A Beginner's Guide to Astrophysics and Cosmology

What the universe is, how we know, and why any of it bends light. Sixteen
chapters, no calculus, written for the same reader as
[the main guide](../guide/index.md) — on the assumption that he knows nothing
about astronomy at all.

!!! abstract "Why this book exists"
    The main guide, *From Calculus to the Money Number*, says **"elliptical
    galaxy" 39 times** and **"cluster" 35 times** without ever defining either.
    Across all 29 of its chapters, these appear **zero** times:

    > Big Bang · CMB · cosmic web · distance ladder · standard candle ·
    > parallax · Milky Way · solar system · Hubble tension

    "Dark matter" appears in one subordinate clause. The guide reproduces a
    cluster mass of $4.6\times10^{13}\,M_\odot$ and cannot tell you whether that
    is a lot, because there is no Milky Way anywhere in the book to compare it
    against.

    It is an excellent guide to the *mathematics* of this program. It uses the
    universe without ever describing it. This is the layer beneath it.

## The gap, precisely { #the-gap }

The sharpest example is redshift — the quantity the whole program runs on.

[Ch. 12](../guide/12-spectroscopy.md#measuring-redshift) opens by promising *"a
redshift $z$, which tells you how far away something is"* — then immediately
takes it back: *"This is a definition, not yet a physical claim… treat $z$ as a
wavelength ratio you read off a spectrum, full stop."*
[Ch. 13](../guide/13-expansion.md#redshift-is-expansion) gives you
$a = 1/(1+z)$ and denies the velocity reading.
[Ch. 15](../guide/15-distances.md#three-distances) hands you an integral.

**No chapter ever says the plain thing**: bigger $z$ means the universe was
smaller then, which means the light left longer ago, which means it is farther
away. [Chapter 8](08-redshift.md) says it, and shows you that $z = 0.5$ — the
guide's standard lens — is light that left 5 billion years ago, when everything
was two-thirds its present size.

## How to read it { #how-to-read }

Front to back, then open the main guide at
[Ch. 9](../guide/09-units.md) and keep going. It is short — a weekend, not a
term — and every chapter ends with an **Unlocks** box naming exactly which guide
chapter it just made readable, and what that chapter had been assuming.

There is **no calculus here.** Arithmetic, ratios, powers of ten, and four
formulas worth memorising: $z = \Delta\lambda/\lambda$, $v = H_0 d$, the
inverse-square law, and $F = GMm/r^2$. Where a point needs a derivative, this
book states the result and hands you to the chapter that derives it. That handoff
is the whole design.

## The ruler { #the-ruler }

One device runs through the whole book. The Milky Way — $10^{11}$ stars,
$10^5$ light-years across — is established in
[Chapter 1](01-scale-ladder.md#our-galaxy) and then used as the unit of
comparison for everything after it. The main guide has no ruler at all, which is
why its numbers float. Here, a cluster is not "$4.6\times10^{13}\,M_\odot$" — it
is *thirty Milky Ways*, and you can feel that.

## What this book will not do { #honesty }

**It will not oversell what is known.** Nobody knows what dark matter *is*.
Nobody knows why the two best measurements of $H_0$ disagree. Nobody knows why
the isothermal conspiracy holds. Where the field is stuck, this book says so —
the same way the guide's own campaign ledger retracts its own numbers.

**It will not simplify in ways you would have to unlearn.** Redshift is *not* a
Doppler shift, and the main guide will contradict you in
[Ch. 13](../guide/13-expansion.md#redshift-is-expansion) if you believe it is.
Where an analogy breaks — and the raisin bread does break — this book says where.
